- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): a technique (also known as grounding) used to improve the quality, accuracy, and freshness of AI responses. To do this, it relies on our core Search ranking systems to extract relevant and up-to-date web pages from our Search index. Our systems review the specific information from those pages to generate a more reliable and useful response. In addition, they display prominent, clickable links to relevant web pages that support the information in the response.
- Query fan-out: a set of concurrent, related queries generated by the model to request more information and fetch additional relevant search results to address the user's query. For example, if the original user's query is 'how to fix a lawn that is full of weeds', fanout queries might include 'best herbicides for lawns', 'remove weeds without chemicals', and 'how to prevent weeds in lawn'.
- Providing a unique point of view: Our AI systems take a look at a variety of sources, so it can be helpful to have a unique viewpoint that stands out. For example, a first-hand review provides a unique perspective based on personal experience, whereas a summary of existing content simply restates information already available elsewhere. Create the content yourself based on what you know about the topic, and consider what in-depth experience you can bring to your content. Don't just recycle what others on the internet have already said, or could easily be produced by a generative AI model.
- Creating non-commodity content that's helpful, reliable, and people-first: Be sure that you're writing non-commodity content that your readers will find helpful and reliable. Commodity content (for example, something like '7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers') is often based on common knowledge, which could originate from anyone, and typically adds little unique insight for readers. In contrast, non-commodity content (such as 'Why We Waived the Inspection & Saved Money: A Look Inside the Sewer Line') provides unique expert or experienced takes that go beyond common knowledge and the ordinary.

## Google Search's guidance on using generative AI content on your website

Generative AI can be especially useful for researching a topic and adding structure to original content. However, using generative AI tools or other similar tools with the goal of generating many pages without adding value for users can violate Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse. If you use generative AI content on your website, make sure it meets the standards of the Search Essentials and our spam policies.

### Focus on accuracy, quality, and relevance

When you create content for the web, focus on accuracy, quality, and relevance, especially when automatically generating the content. This includes metadata like `<title>` elements, `<meta>` description elements, structured data, and image alt text, which can appear in search results.

For structured data, also ensure compliance with the general guidelines, the specific policies for the individual search features, and validate the markup to ensure eligibility for Search features.

### Give users context

Sharing information about how a piece of content was created can help give your readers more context. If you automatically generate content, consider adding information on how your content was created in a way that makes sense for your audience, such as by providing more background information on how automation was used and adding image metadata.

For ecommerce sites, Google Merchant Center has policies for AI-generated content. In particular, AI-generated images must contain metadata with the `DigitalSourceType` `TrainedAlgorithmicMedia` metadata. AI-generated product data, such as title and description attributes, must be specified separately and labeled as AI-generated.

URL: <https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content?hl=en>

## Expanded notes from official English Google Search Central pages

### Search Essentials (expanded summary)

The Google Search Essentials are the core parts of what makes web-based content eligible to appear and perform well on Google Search. They are made up of three pillars:

- **Technical requirements**: What Google needs from a web page to show it in Google Search.
- **Spam policies**: The behaviors and tactics that can lead to a page or site being ranked lower or omitted entirely.
- **Key best practices**: The main things that can help improve how a site appears in Google Search results.

It does not cost any money to appear in Google Search results. Meeting all requirements and best practices does not mean that Google will crawl, index, or serve a page. Sites that focus on the best content and experience for people and uphold the spirit of the principles are more likely to do well.

Source: [Google Search Essentials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials?hl=en)

### Google Search technical requirements (expanded summary)

A page is eligible to be indexed by Google Search when it meets three minimum requirements:

1. Googlebot isn't blocked from finding and accessing the page.
2. The page works and returns an HTTP `200 (success)` status code.
3. The page has indexable content, meaning the text is in a supported file type and the content doesn't violate spam policies.

Google only indexes publicly accessible pages. If a page requires a login, Googlebot cannot crawl it. Pages blocked by `robots.txt` are unlikely to show in Search results, but a URL might still appear if it is linked from elsewhere. To tell Google not to index a page, use `noindex` and allow Google to crawl the URL.

Source: [Google Search technical requirements](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/technical?hl=en)

### Spam policies for Google web search (expanded summary)

In the context of Google Search, spam refers to techniques used to deceive users or manipulate Search systems into featuring content prominently. Spam policies help protect users and improve result quality. Common spam policies include:

- **Cloaking**: Presenting different content to users and search engines to manipulate rankings or mislead users.
- **Doorway abuse**: Creating pages or sites to rank for similar queries that funnel users to less useful intermediate pages.
- **Expired domain abuse**: Buying an expired domain and repurposing it primarily to manipulate rankings with low-value content.
- **Hacked content**: Content placed on a site without permission, often through code or page injection or malicious redirects.
- **Hidden text and link abuse**: Placing content on a page in a way solely to manipulate search engines and not to be easily viewable by people.
- **Keyword stuffing**: Filling a page with keywords or numbers in an attempt to manipulate rankings.
- **Link spam**: Creating links to or from a site primarily to manipulate search rankings.
- **Machine-generated traffic**: Sending automated queries to Google without permission.
- **Malicious practices**: Creating a mismatch between user expectations and the actual outcome, leading to deceptive or harmful experiences.
- **Misleading functionality**: Creating sites that trick users into thinking they can access content or services but in reality cannot.
- **Scaled content abuse**: Generating many pages for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users.
- **Scraping**: Taking content from other sites and hosting it to manipulate rankings.
- **Site reputation abuse**: Publishing third-party content on a host site mainly because of the host's established ranking signals.
- **Sneaky redirects**: Redirecting users or search engines maliciously to show unexpected content.
- **Thin affiliation**: Publishing product affiliate content copied directly from the original merchant without added value.
- **User-generated spam**: Spammy content added by users through channels intended for user content.

Source: [Spam policies for Google web search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies?hl=en)

### SEO Starter Guide (expanded summary)

The SEO starter guide is for website owners who want to improve their site's presence in Google Search. It explains that Google is a fully automated search engine and that most sites are found automatically. Key topics include:

- Helping Google find your content through links and sitemaps.
- Making sure Google can see your page the same way a user does.
- Organizing your site with descriptive URLs, directories for similar topics, and reduced duplicate content.
- Creating interesting and useful content that is easy to read, unique, up to date, and helpful, reliable, and people-first.
- Expecting your readers' search terms and writing naturally.
- Avoiding distracting advertisements and intrusive interstitials.
- Influencing title links with good `<title>` elements and snippets with good meta descriptions.
- Adding and optimizing images and videos with `alt` text and relevant nearby text.
- Promoting your site through legitimate channels without overdoing it.
- Myths and things not to focus on, such as meta keywords, keyword stuffing, exact word counts, subdomain versus directory debates, and heading order.

Source: [SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide?hl=en)

### In-depth guide to how Google Search works (expanded summary)

Google Search is a fully automated search engine that uses web crawlers to explore the web. The vast majority of pages in results are found and added automatically, not manually submitted. Google Search works in three stages:

1. **Crawling**: Google downloads text, images, and videos from pages it has found. Googlebot discovers URLs from known pages, extracted links, and sitemaps. It renders pages and runs JavaScript using a recent version of Chrome.
2. **Indexing**: Google analyzes the content, selects canonical versions, and stores information in the Google index.
3. **Serving search results**: Google returns information relevant to the user's query, using hundreds of factors such as location, language, and device.

Not all pages make it through each stage. Common issues include server or network problems, `robots.txt` rules blocking Googlebot, `noindex` meta rules, and low-quality content. Google doesn't accept payment to crawl a site more frequently or to rank it higher.

Source: [How Google Search works](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/how-search-works?hl=en)

### Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content (expanded summary)

Google's automated ranking systems prioritize helpful, reliable information created to benefit people, not content created to manipulate rankings. Site owners should self-assess their content against content and quality questions and expertise questions. The page also explains:

- How to focus on people-first content.
- How to avoid creating search engine-first content.
- E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness), especially trust, and the quality rater guidelines.
- The "Who, How, and Why" framework: who created the content, how it was created, and why it was created.

For topics that could significantly impact health, financial stability, or safety, or the welfare of society (Your Money or Your Life topics), strong E-E-A-T is especially important.

Source: [Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content?hl=en)

### Optimizing your website for generative AI features on Google Search (expanded summary)

SEO remains relevant for generative AI search because Google's generative AI features rely on core Search ranking and quality systems. Key concepts include:

- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: Uses core Search ranking to retrieve relevant, up-to-date pages and generate reliable responses with prominent, clickable links.
- **Query fan-out**: Generates related concurrent queries to gather additional relevant results.

Recommendations include creating valuable, non-commodity content with unique points of view; organizing content for human readers; adding high-quality images and video; building a clear technical structure; optimizing local business and ecommerce details; and measuring visibility with the Generative AI performance report in Search Console.

Myths to ignore include creating `llms.txt` files, chunking content into tiny pieces, rewriting content just for AI, seeking inauthentic mentions, and overfocusing on structured data. AEO and GEO are not separate disciplines for Google Search; optimizing for generative AI is still SEO.

Source: [Optimizing your website for generative AI features on Google Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide?hl=en)

### Maintaining your website's SEO (expanded summary)

This guide covers in-depth SEO tasks such as controlling how Google crawls and indexes the site. Topics include duplicate content and canonicalization, accessible resources, `robots.txt`, sitemaps, international and multilingual sites with `hreflang`, site moves and migrations, and crawl budget. It also covers helping Google understand the site through text and structured data, following content-specific guidelines for video, images, news, and other content types, and managing the user experience with HTTPS, speed, mobile-friendliness, and Core Web Vitals.

Source: [Maintain your site's SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/get-started?hl=en)

### Do you need an SEO? (expanded summary)

Hiring an SEO can improve a site's visibility or risk damage. Good SEOs provide services such as reviewing site content and structure, technical advice, content development, keyword research, and more. When hiring, ask for examples of previous work, whether they follow the Google Search Essentials, expected results and timeframes, and how they measure success. Check references and be wary of guaranteed rankings, "special relationships" with Google, or unsolicited emails. No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google.

Source: [Do you need an SEO?](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo?hl=en)

### Google Search's guidance on using third-party SEO tools, services, and advice (expanded summary)

Evaluate any third-party advice against official Google guidance. Think critically about tools and services that generate sitemaps, set indexing directives, create "SEO-optimized" content, promise ranking improvements, or sell "AEO" or "GEO" services. No third-party tool has access to Google's internal ranking or AI systems, and Google does not evaluate or endorse third-party SEO tools. Use Search Console as the first-party source of data.

Source: [Third-party SEO tools and advice](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/third-party-seo?hl=en)

### Google Search's guidance on using generative AI content on your website (expanded summary)

Generative AI can be useful for research and adding structure to original content, but using it to generate many pages without adding value can violate the scaled content abuse spam policy. AI-generated content must meet Search Essentials and spam policies. Focus on accuracy, quality, and relevance, including for `<title>`, meta descriptions, structured data, and image `alt` text. Give users context about how content was created. For ecommerce, AI-generated images and product data must be labeled appropriately.

Source: [Guidance on using generative AI](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content?hl=en)

### Get started with Search: a developer's guide (expanded summary)

This guide helps developers make sites work well with Google Search. Key points include testing how Google sees the site with the URL Inspection tool and Rich Results Test, using crawlable `<a>` links and sitemaps, accounting for JavaScript rendering, keeping content in the DOM, using meaningful HTTP status codes, consolidating duplicate URLs and localizing versions, using `robots.txt`, `noindex`, and password protection appropriately, and enabling rich results with structured data.

Source: [Get started with Search: a developer's guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/get-started-developers?hl=en)
